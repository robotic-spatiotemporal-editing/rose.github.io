Robotic Spatiotemporal Editing: A Unified Interface for Controllable Action Generation via Masked Action Reconstruction

Abstract

Generative policies can synthesize robot behaviors, yet controlling and editing them through diverse spatiotemporal references remains challenging. Real-world manipulation requires robots to satisfy user-specified constraints on where, when, and how actions are executed. Our key insight is that spatiotemporal control references, such as keypoints, flows, orientations, and keyposes, can be viewed as partial specifications of the same temporal Cartesian action space. We introduce the MAP (Masked Action Space) interface to unify these action references and formulate controllable manipulation as an action inpainting problem. Our Inpainting Policy (IP) reconstructs missing action dimensions of the end-effector, combining progress-guided alignment with multi-mask training to support unseen action constraint combinations. We further adopt a training-free RePaint variant that iteratively inpaints the action during denoising to reduce control error. On ManiSkill and LIBERO-10, our policy achieves average task success rates of 92.67% and 64.67%, respectively. Real-world experiments demonstrate spatiotemporal control with average positional and rotational errors of 43.3 mm and 7.8°, respectively.

PROGRESS-GUIDED SLIDING WINDOW(1) A progress-guided sliding window aligns MAP with the current task progress and provides a temporally anchored MAP condition.

MULTI-MODAL JOINT TRAINING(2) Multi-modal joint training over heterogeneous mask families enables structured control and zero-shot generalization to unseen spatiotemporal compositions.

REPAINT TRAINING-FREE INFERENCE STRATEGY(3) We adopt RePaint (Lugmayr et al., 2022) as a training-free inference strategy, which repeatedly reinserts the specified action tokens during the denoising process of the diffusion-based policy, thereby improving adherence to the provided constraints.

Joint-Training Performance. Table 1 shows that STC performs worse than its corresponding base policies, indicating that directly conditioning action generation on control signals can interfere with task SR. In contrast, Inpainting Policy improves task completion while achieving lower control error, performing well in both multi-task and single-task settings and achieving the highest average success rates of 64.67% and 92.67%, respectively. The training-free RePaint variant further reduces CE with relative reductions of 42.2% and 39.1%, respectively, demonstrating a trade-off between control accuracy and task completion.

Figure 5: Mask-type breakdown in joint training. Left: We compare success rates across reference types, including the unseen mixed mask Mix0, to evaluate controllability and compositional generalization. Right: Control error decreases as training progresses.

Mask-Type Breakdown and Training Dynamics. Figure 5 shows that Inpainting Policy provides the strongest average control performance across all control types, especially on the unseen Mix0 mask (80.56%). STC performs strongly on dense 2D/3D flow (80.00%), but degrades sharply under sparse constraints. The right plot shows that Inpainting Policy progressively improves its control capability during training, with CE decreasing from approximately 0.049 to 0.022. In contrast, RePaint maintains the lowest CE, around 0.014–0.015, across all checkpoints. Notably, since the Mix0 composition is absent from the training set, its high success rate indicates that the policy can compose learned mask patterns without additional finetuning.

(b) We compare mask types with approximately equal numbers of spatiotemporal tokens, alongside a base policy (a DiT policy), to assess how constraint structure influences the performance of Inpainting Policy. Here, r denotes the visible ratio, i.e., the proportion of action information that is not masked in MAP.

Mask Ratio and Mask-Type Analysis. Figure 4(a) shows that increasing the visible-action ratio generally improves task success, indicating that additional known action components provide stronger guidance for reconstructing executable behaviors. For the structured 3D-point mask, success increases from 57.0% at a ratio of 0.02 to 91.7% at 0.50. Although the random mask also improves with more visible actions, its performance is less stable and reaches only 71.7% at the highest ratio. This gap suggests that structured control signals convey more task-relevant information than randomly selected action components. Figure 4(b) compares different mask types under approximately the same number of visible spatiotemporal tokens. Across all mask types, IP outperforms the unconditioned DiT policy. Positional and full-pose constraints provide the strongest guidance, whereas orientation-only constraints are less informative for task completion. The random-mask result further shows that semantically structured action references are more effective than an equivalent number of arbitrary visible tokens.

Result. As shown in Figure 6, with three or more reference points, the policy achieves success rates above 93%, while the cup-to-reference-point distance ranges from 18.2 to 63.7 mm, with an average of 43.3 mm. In the liquid-pouring task, the tube-tilt error remains below 8°, with a mean of 7.8°. These results show that providing explicit control constraints substantially improves task completion and reference adherence: denser references produce smoother motion and smaller deviations from the target. In contrast, when only a single reference is provided at the final progress point, the task completion rate falls below 30%, revealing a limitation of sparse late-stage supervision and an important direction for improving the subsequent editing system.
